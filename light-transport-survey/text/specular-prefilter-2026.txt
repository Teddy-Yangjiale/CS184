Abstract
Neural Radiance Caching (NRC) [MRNK21] provides an online neural representation of scene illumination for real-time path
tracing. This paper presents an NRC variant tailored to specular lighting through a reflection-direction parameterization and
a roughness-dependent radiance target. Our network predicts prefiltered incoming radiance at individual surface points, which
is combined with a precomputed BRDF integration map using the split-sum approximation to estimate outgoing radiance. On
Bunny and Specular Sponza, the reported comparisons indicate faster convergence to the accumulated path-tracing reference
and improved specular-lighting quality relative to the evaluated NRC baselines. Our online-trained method maintains real-time
performance while adapting its cache during rendering.

1. Introduction
Specular reflection depends strongly on viewing direction and sur-
face roughness, making its representation challenging for radiance
caching. Neural Radiance Caching (NRC) [MRNK21] learns scene
illumination online to accelerate path tracing. We target specular
lighting by parameterizing the cache with the reflected viewing di-
rection and learning roughness-dependent prefiltered incoming ra-
diance for split-sum reconstruction.
The main goal of path-tracing is to calculate the solution of Light
Transport Equation (LTE) [Kaj86]:

Here, Le is the radiance emitted in direction ωo, Li is the radiance
arriving from direction ωi, and fs is the bidirectional scattering dis-
tribution function (BSDF). The angle θi is measured between the
surface normal n at position x and ωi. We omit the position argu-
ment for brevity. For reflection, the BSDF is restricted to a bidirec-
tional reflectance distribution function (BRDF). In this work, we
consider its specular component. Estimating the integral is compu-
tationally expensive, motivating approaches that cache previously
computed lighting information.
Traditional radiance caching approaches like [KBPZ06] (and
analogues) are based on several heuristics that are difficult to im-
plement and tend to produce artefacts that need special care to be
suppressed [SJJ12]. Other progressive rendering and reconstruction
approaches (for example [MCY14]) also uses handcrafted mathe-
matic models which makes their implementation and testing inside
and existing rendering system a challenging task. At the same time
NRC [MRNK21] reduces such complexity by way of abstraction
of internal radiance cache representation via neural network. This
method represents scene illumination with a multilayer perceptron
that maps hit ray positions and directions to radiance values of the
scattered light. The approach is lightweight and it is designed to
continuously train in real-time to support dynamic scenes while en-
suring predictable performance and resource usage. The research
presents a multitude of methods to regulate and stabilize the output
of the neural network like self-training and path termination that
are omitted in our paper. The idea of encoding input data was also
explored in these papers [MESK22] [VHM∗22] [MMR∗19].
Our method parameterizes the cache to represent specular reflec-
tion, including mirror-like and rough glossy reflection. Following
the reflection-direction parameterization used in [VHM∗22], we
use the viewing direction reflected about the surface normal as a
network input. We also use the split-sum approximation [KG13] to
separate prefiltered lighting from the final BRDF reconstruction.
The cache predicts roughness-dependent filtered radiance, while
the BRDF integration lookup supplies the final Fresnel-dependent
reconstruction. Together, these terms approximate the reflected
component of Equation (1).
2. Previous Work
2.1. Microfacet Specular BRDF
Microfacet theory is a popular approach to modelling surface re-
flection and transmission. In this theory, surfaces are modelled as
a collection of small microfacets, with their aggregated statistical
behaviour determining the observed scattering of light. The micro-
facet specular BRDF is an expression that describes the scatter-
ing of light from a surface composed of microfacets. To compute
BRDF from such a model, local lighting effects at the microfacet
level need to be considered, such as microfacet occlusion, shad-
owing, and interreflection. An expression representing BRDF con-
sists of two statistical measures, a microfacet normalized distribu-
tion function(NDF) of facets represented as D and a shadowing-
masking function between facets G, and a Fresnel reflectance fac-
tor F. All of that is combined in a general Cook-Torrance [CT81]
[CT82] microfacet specular shading model. Here h is the halfway-

2.3. Neural radiance cache
We examined various methods employing neural networks (and
multilayer perceptron specifically) for providing scene informa-
tion. Neural methods are increasingly being used for rendering
in various applications. The recent method of spatio-directional
neural representations replaces traditional discrete representations
with an MLP for reconstructing 3D geometry-aware solutions. The
most widely adopted strategy currently is Neural Radiance Fields
[MST∗20], the science publication of which inspired a multitude of
works [BMT∗21] [VHM∗22] in the novel view synthesis problem.
Gradually, the interest in neural approaches for global illumina-
tion increased. There has been a significant effort to improve sam-
pling [MMR∗19] and denoising of Monte Carlo global illumina-
tion algorithms [BVM∗17] [CKS∗17] [GLA∗19]. The current work
is based on NRC [MRNK21] where researchers use small neural
networks that undergo online training to store and predict indirect
global illumination for real-time path tracing. Further development
shows the significant advantage of using hash grid parametric en-
coding for NRC [MESK22].
3. Proposed method
Our method uses a single multilayer perceptron to predict pre-
filtered incoming radiance at a queried position, reflection direc-
tion, and roughness. The network is trained during rendering to
adapt to scene changes. We divide rendering into three passes.
The first generates training paths with multiple surface bounces.
The second traces rendering paths and stores the surface informa-
tion needed to query the cache, terminating paths earlier than the
training pass. Both passes use Monte Carlo integration and impor-
tance sampling for continuation directions. The third queries the
cache and reconstructs outgoing radiance using the BRDF integra-
tion lookup.
For each surface hit, we collect the radiance and path-throughput
information along the continuation of the training path. These data
are used to reconstruct an RGB incoming-radiance target Y, which
is stored in the training buffer together with its cache query. Differ-
ent sampled paths can produce different targets for the same query.
The cache query is q = (φ(x),ωrefl,ρ), where x is the surface
position, φ is its hash-grid encoding, and ρ is the surface rough-
ness. For an outgoing direction ωo pointing away from the surface,
the reflected direction is ωrefl = 2(n · ωo)n −ωo. We hash-grid en-
code positions [MESK22] and pass reflection direction and rough-
ness directly to the MLP. Each query produces an RGB estimate of
the filtered incoming radiance. The roughness input conditions the
cached lighting on the reflection lobe.

squared-error loss, the population-optimal predictor is the condi-

The sampling distribution and target weighting therefore determine

during online adaptation, we use an exponential moving average of
the optimized network weights. The final pass combines the pre-

outgoing specular radiance in the camera direction. This recon-
struction retains the split-sum approximation and its grazing-angle
limitations.
4. Experiments and Results
We use the ray-tracing pipeline in Falcor [BYC∗20] and the MLP
implementation in tiny-cuda-nn [Mü21]. All images were rendered
at a resolution of 1920×1080 on an Intel Core i7-8700K processor
with an NVIDIA RTX 3080 Ti graphics card. We evaluate image
quality using FLIP [ANAM∗20], SSIM [WBSS04], and MSE on
each rendering frame. MSE is displayed on a logarithmic scale be-
cause its values span a wide range. The reference image is obtained
by accumulating the regular path tracer over 4000 frames.
Our findings indicate that the modifications have not resulted in
any discernible performance degradation while improving conver-
gence and output quality. The BRDF integration map is precom-
puted and queried during rendering. The changes to the neural net-
work inputs do not negatively influence the other major NRC com-
ponents.
We compare our method with the NRC baseline and an NRC
variant trained only on initial hits. We include the initial-hit vari-
ant because removing training targets from multiple hits along a
path reduced NRC output quality in our tests. The two variants are
distinguished in the comparison plots.
Figure 1 presents a qualitative comparison after 1000 rendering
frames. The displayed images were adjusted for improved visibil-
ity. The FLIP results indicate closer agreement with the accumu-
lated reference for our method than for the evaluated NRC base-
lines. Figure 2 shows the evolution of the quality metrics dur-
ing training and indicates faster convergence for our method. On
Bunny, our method achieves better image quality than the NRC
baseline after 1000 frames.
5. Conclusion
We presented an NRC variant for real-time specular-lighting ap-
proximation that predicts roughness-dependent prefiltered incom-
ing radiance and reconstructs outgoing radiance using the split-sum
approximation. On Bunny and Specular Sponza, the reported com-
parisons show faster convergence and improved output quality rel-
ative to the evaluated NRC baselines while maintaining the same
level of performance. The evaluated method uses hash-grid position
encoding, a reflection-direction input, and split-sum reconstruction.
It retains the grazing-angle limitations of the split-sum approxima-
tion. Online training enables the cache to adapt during rendering,
motivating further investigation of its behavior in dynamic scenes.
